Group 3 · What you do with clothes — phrasal verbs

Core · SB Unit 1 · p. 11
Three rules
🔀SEPARABLE — OBJECT IS A NOUN

With a separable phrasal verb, a noun object can go after the particle or in between — both are correct.

  • I tried on the red jacket.
  • I tried the red jacket on.
  • She took back the dress. = She took the dress back.
📌OBJECT IS A PRONOUN — ALWAYS IN THE MIDDLE

A pronoun (it, them, him …) must go between the verb and the particle.

  • I tried it on. I tried on it.
  • Take them off. Take off them.
  • She gave it back. She gave back it.
🔗THREE-WORD VERBS — NEVER SEPARATED

A three-word phrasal verb keeps the order verb + particle + particle + object.

  • get away with casual stuff
  • keep up with the latest fashions
  • fit in with my colleagues
Corpus spot
  • The verb in a phrasal verb is still irregular in the past tense: took off (not taked off), went out, stood out, put together.

Group 5 · Comparing & choosing

Core · SB Unit 1 · pp. 12–13 · Grammar folder p. 188
Six rules
📏-ER / MORE
  • One-syllable adjectives add -er / -est.
  • Adjectives of two or more syllables use more / the most.
  • Spelling — ending in -e: just add -r / -st.
  • Spelling — vowel + consonant: double the consonant.
  • Spelling — consonant + y: change to -ier / -iest.
  • old → older → the oldest
  • nice → nicer → the nicest
  • big → bigger · slim → slimmer
  • dirty → dirtier · skinny → the skinniest
  • casual → more casual → the most casual
🔁BOTH FORMS ARE CORRECT
  • Some two-syllable adjectives can take either -er/-est or more/most.
  • simple → simpler / more simple
  • common · likely · narrow
  • pleasant · polite · gentle
⚠️IRREGULAR
  • Don't add -er.
  • Don't add more.
  • good → better → the best
  • bad → worse → the worst
  • far → farther / further → the farthest / furthest
  • old → older / elder → the oldest / eldest
📉LESS / THE LEAST · NOT AS … AS
  • less … than and the least … are the opposite of more / the most.
  • not as … as = not as … as (not so … as is rarer).
  • less serious than · less expensive than

  • Sally is not as tall as her brother.
  • These shoes don't look as comfortable as the flip flops.
🎚️ADVERBS OF DEGREE
  • Before a comparative: a bit · slightly · a little · a lot · much · far · rather · a great deal.
  • Before a superlative: by far · easily · much · quite.

  • Adverbs ending in -ly: more / less + adverb.
  • Short adverbs add -er.
  • Some adverbs are irregular.

  • less seriously · less exclusively

Exercise 8

Choose 1 of 3 options and defend it using comparative structures

Situation3 options
A — The hospital is choosing new uniforms for doctorsa traditional white coat · hospital scrubs for everyone · smart everyday clothes with a name badge
B — Your friend is going to a wedding with very little moneybuy a new outfit online · rent a designer dress for the day · wear something she already has and add new accessories
Requirements
  • 1 opening sentence stating your choice (Out of the three options, I'd go for … / Given the choice, I'd …)
  • comparative + adverb of degree (far / much / a lot / slightly)
  • not as … as or nowhere near as … as
  • a superlative with by far or easily
  • 1 concession sentence (Admittedly, … However, …) about the other two options
  • 1 closing sentence (On balance, … / The best option would be to …)

Word count: 0

Model A · dress code

Out of the three options, I'd go for scrubs for everyone, and I'll explain why. The main reason is hygiene. Scrubs are loose-fitting, they're washed by the hospital every day, and they're by far the most practical option when you're on your feet for twelve hours. They're also much cheaper for staff than a wardrobe of smart clothes, and nobody has to weigh up the options every morning — you just put them on.

Admittedly, a white coat presents a more professional image, and many patients still expect it. However, coats are worn for days, so they're nowhere near as clean as scrubs. Smart everyday clothes with a name badge look friendly and let people express their personality, but they're the least practical choice: a silk blouse or a good pair of trousers is ruined the first time a patient is sick on it, and staff would end up spending a fortune on clothes.

On balance, I've decided on scrubs because they're twice as practical as the other two options and only slightly less smart. The best option would be to give everyone two sets in the same colour, so the team looks well-dressed and, more importantly, safe.

Model B · wedding

Given the choice, I'd advise her to go for the third option — wear something she already has and add new accessories. It's a lot cheaper than the other two, and a stylish necklace or a new pair of shoes can change the look of an outfit completely. She could splash out on one good item instead of a whole outfit — in my experience, people notice accessories far more than the dress itself.

Admittedly, buying online is convenient and there's a huge choice. However, it's the riskiest option: the dress might not fit, it might be badly made, and there's no time to take it back before the wedding. Renting a designer dress sounds elegant, and it's far less expensive than buying one, but she'd worry all evening about spilling something on it — nowhere near as relaxing as wearing her own clothes.

All things considered, the third option is the best value for money: she'd look presentable, she wouldn't spend a fortune, and she'd feel comfortable as well as stylish. If she really wants something new, the best way to do it is to save up and buy one well-made dress after the wedding, when the sales start.
